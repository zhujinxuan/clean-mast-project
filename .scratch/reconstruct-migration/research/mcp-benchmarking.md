## Q1 — Resampling design for autocorrelated wind time series

**Row-level (i.i.d.) resampling is invalid for autocorrelated series; contiguous blocking is the standard correction.**

- The block bootstrap family exists precisely because i.i.d. bootstrap fails under dependence: Künsch (1989) introduced the moving-block jackknife/bootstrap for dependent data; Politis & Romano (1992; 1994) the circular block and stationary bootstrap. Politis & White (2004, corrected by Patton, Politis & White 2009) give the automatic block-length selector; the optimal block length scales as $b^* \propto n^{1/3}$ (implemented, e.g., in R `blocklength`, Stashevsky 2021, and Duke code by A. Patton, https://public.econ.duke.edu/~ap172/ppw.R.txt).
- For **model selection** rather than variance estimation, the cross-validation literature reaches the same verdict: leave-one-out CV is inconsistent for dependent data (Shao 1993, via Racine 2000); Racine (2000) proposes hv-block CV (contiguous held-out blocks with gaps) as asymptotically optimal for stationary dependent observations. Bergmeir & Benítez (2012, Information Sciences 191:192–213, doi:10.1016/j.ins.2011.12.028) show standard CV violates temporal dependence in theory, and empirically advocate **blocked CV** for time series; Cerqueira, Torgo & Mozetič (2020, Machine Learning 109, doi:10.1007/s10994-020-05910-7) confirm blocked CV variants are the best performers for stationary series.

**Wind-specific practice: sliding contiguous windows sized by the seasonal cycle, not by statistical optimality.**

- Weekes & Tomlin (2014a) evaluate 3-month-trained MCP with a sliding-window scheme: a 1-year training **window** shifted in 1-month steps over an 11-year record → 120 replicates, training and test never overlapping, and "the test period always covers an integer number of years thus avoiding seasonal variations in the test data".
- Basse et al. (2021, WES 6:1473, doi:10.5194/wes-6-1473-2021) use 90-day periods with a 3-day sliding step → 122 periods per site, with wrap-around "so that all seasons are considered equally".
- The $n^{1/3}$ block-length rule (≈ hours–days for hourly wind data) targets variance estimation, not the preservation of the seasonal structure that dominates MCP error; wind practitioners therefore size blocks by season/month, not by the asymptotic formula.
- Why month-scale blocks: the seasonal cycle is the dominant systematic-error driver. Basse et al. (2021) show mean-wind-speed bias from sub-year training is seasonal by construction (their Eq. 13: bias = representativeness of the measurement period − β1-weighted representativeness of the reference), and that even the site–reference correlation coefficient has a seasonal cycle (lower in summer). Sheridan et al. (2025, WES 10:1451, doi:10.5194/wes-10-1451-2025) found July–August the worst single months for MCP corrections across 66 US sites.
- Caveat on fold independence: Bodini et al. (2016, WES 1:115–128, doi:10.5194/wes-1-115-2016) show year-to-year persistence (serial correlation across years) widens true uncertainty ~3× versus an independence assumption. Monthly folds within one year share the same inter-annual regime; they are balanced seasonal samples, not independent replicates — naive standard errors across folds will be optimistic.

**Contiguous vs stratified.** Every cited scheme holds out *contiguous* blocks. Stratified row/month sampling without contiguity would both break synoptic continuity (the dependence the blocking is there to respect) and unbalance seasonal coverage — no cited MCP study does it.

## Q2 — Selection metrics: WS RMSE vs energy-based; bias guardrails; direction

**The literature evaluates both value-by-value and distribution/energy statistics, and the two can rank methods differently.**

- Rogers et al. (2005, JWIA 93(3):243–264, doi:10.1016/j.jweia.2004.12.002) evaluated each MCP algorithm on six aspects spanning mean wind speed, wind speed distribution, AEP/capacity factor via a sample turbine power curve, and wind-direction distribution.
- Weekes & Tomlin (2014a): metrics = mean wind speed, mean wind power density, standard deviation, Weibull shape factor, each with MAE / %error / mean bias error. Best MCP over 22 sites: 0.21 m/s (4.8%) error in mean wind speed but 14% in wind power density — i.e. **mean-based metrics barely discriminate methods; energy/distribution metrics do** (all three tested methods predicted the mean similarly well; LR2/VR beat plain LR specifically on power and distribution).
- Basse et al. (2021): error scores = bias in annual mean wind speed, bias in variance, bias in theoretical AEP of a 3.2 MW turbine (robust to power-curve choice; they tested 1.8/4.2 MW too). Key quantitative result: via error propagation on $E[u^3]$, the relative error in the mean is weighted ~6× the relative error in variance — **mean bias dominates energy error**.
- Jonietz Alvarez et al. (2024, WES 9:2217–2233, doi:10.5194/wes-9-2217-2024) use both families side by side: value-by-value RMSE plus overall statistics (mean wind speed difference, chi-squared distribution error, mean wind-direction difference), noting RMSE and R² are "the most widely used" value-by-value statistics.
- Bias guardrails: Basse et al. (2021) show the two standard linear methods carry **opposite-sign seasonal biases** (VR overestimates for summer training and underestimates for winter; LR-with-residuals mostly the reverse) — bias is systematic, method-dependent, and season-dependent, so a bias criterion in selection is well-founded. Practice also gates on site–reference correlation before any LTC (Basse et al. 2021 discuss r thresholds; some reanalysis pairs dropped below r = 0.5 in summer).
- Energy metrics require a power curve (Basse: Enercon 3.2 MW; Rogers: sample turbine curves; Sheridan: capacity factor), and conclusions were shown to be robust to curve choice — supporting "wind-power RMSE only when a reference turbine curve is configured".
- Direction: Rogers et al. (2005) scored direction-distribution quality; Jonietz Alvarez et al. (2024) use mean wind-direction difference and treat sector-average-deviation (SAD) as the standard direction MCP method (cf. SpeedSort, King & Hurley 2005). No cited study drives *wind-speed* method selection by direction quality; direction is benchmarked as its own track.

## Q3 — Method evidence: variance ratio vs OLS variants vs sectorized

**No dominant method — repeatedly, across 25 years.**

- Rogers, Rogers & Manwell (2005) compared four algorithms — simple linear regression, the Mortimer method (binned distributions of wind-speed ratios), vector regression, and the variance-ratio (VR) method they propose. Findings: LR, Mortimer and VR all gave unbiased capacity-factor estimates with low standard deviations (LR slightly underpredicted CF, ~4%, via Weibull-shape overestimation; vector regression was clearly worst, badly underestimating Weibull scale/cf). VR preserved distribution metrics best.
- Bass et al. (2000), as quoted by Basse et al. (2021): none of the investigated methods stood out; success had "less to do with the mechanics of the methodology itself, and more to do with facets of the data being analysed". Carta, Velázquez & Cabrera (2013, RSER 27:362–400, doi:10.1016/j.rser.2013.07.004) — the standard MCP review — conclude uncertainty depends much more on the (reference) data than on the method, and that linear approaches dominate industrial practice.
- A 2018 German round-robin (Basse et al. 2018, cited in Basse et al. 2021): 24 of 29 consultants used linear correlation methods, which mostly outperformed more complicated approaches.
- Angenent (2023, TU Delft MSc thesis, uuid:02207821-41f7-498d-9aef-b4d3acfbbff3): across 35 sites, the best-vs-worst regression variants differed by only ~0.046 m/s in mean wind speed on average; the (target-site-sector) Matrix method was generally best for the mean.

**Why VR exists, and what it buys:**

- Plain OLS attenuates the predicted variance by the correlation coefficient (predicted σ shrinks by factor r; Weekes & Tomlin 2014a; Basse et al. 2021), biasing distributions and energy. VR sets the slope to σ_target/σ_ref (the r = 1 special case of the OLS slope), preserving first and second moments of the training period; OLS-with-residuals (Weekes & Tomlin's "LR2"; Basse's "LR") instead re-injects Gaussian residual scatter.
- Evidence: VR and OLS-with-residuals beat plain OLS for wind power density and distribution metrics while all are adequate for the mean (Weekes & Tomlin 2014a; confirmed by Rogers et al. 2005 for distribution metrics). But VR is **not uniformly better**: Basse et al. (2021) show VR amplifies seasonal mean bias when the reference overstates the annual cycle (all six reanalyses did), while OLS-with-residuals showed the opposite pattern — method choice interacts with reference-data bias.
- Machine learning: MLR/adaptive splines/regression trees all helped but trees lagged at ≤6 months of training (Sheridan et al. 2025); RF/AdaBoost reduced inter-annual sensitivity vs classic LR in one 12-site study (WES 11:661, 2026, doi:10.5194/wes-11-661-2026); KNN useful for gap filling (Schwegmann et al. 2023 via Jonietz Alvarez et al. 2024); a 2025 review (Houndekindo et al., Energy Convers. Manage., https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0196890425000780) catalogs ML–statistical approaches and leaves capability questions open. Net: ML ≈ linear for long-term means; no evidence it should displace linear methods as defaults.

**Sectorized approaches:**

- Sector-wise regression is the industry norm for year-long fits: "in practical applications, a sectorwise regression is often performed for an LTC of measurement data comprising a whole year… important especially in a complex environment (López et al., 2008)" (Basse et al. 2021). Weekes & Tomlin (2014a) binned by reference wind direction into 30° sectors (12 regressions) with a **global fallback for bins with < 20 entries**; Jonietz Alvarez et al. (2024) also regress per 30° sector and add Gaussian noise per Carta et al.'s (2013) recommendation; SpeedSort fits per direction sector (King & Hurley 2005, via UL Windographer docs).
- Counter-evidence for short records: "For the shorter 3-month periods, sectorwise binning … generally yielded slightly worse results in this study (presumably due to low data coverage in the different direction sectors)" (Basse et al. 2021). So the 1-sector vs 16-sector trade-off is overlap-length-dependent — exactly what a benchmark should decide per mast rather than hardcode.

## Q4 — Long-record vs short-record evaluation conventions

- **12 months on-site is the normative minimum.** Basse et al. (2021): measurements usually ~1 year; ≥12 months is recommended or mandated by FGW e.V. (2020), IEC (2017) and MEASNET (2016); "the use of complete years is important as an uneven representation of different months increases the uncertainty (Taylor et al., 2004; Liléo et al., 2013)". IEC 61400-1:2019 §11.3.4 (checked via the CSA C61400-1:21 preview): the measurement period should cover seasonal variation with a 12-month minimum, monthly coverage should be high, and a measure-correlate-predict procedure may be used to extend the data; long-term assessment of mean/extreme winds is "normally required" when a reliable long-term source exists. MEASNET's "Evaluation of Site-Specific Wind Conditions" (V2 April 2016; V3 2022 per measnet.com/publications and industry summaries) is described by TU Delft AEP theses as requiring ≥1 year on-site with a preferred 10–30-year long-term reference horizon.
- **Availability gates: 80–95% depending on guideline.** FGW 2020 demands > 80%; MEASNET 2016 > 90%; floating-lidar verification (OWA 2018) 95% (all as stated in Jonietz Alvarez et al. 2024). Basse et al.'s own 18-site benchmark corpus had > 80% availability at all sites and > 90% at 14 of 18.
- **When is whole-series evaluation adequate?** The benchmark literature's ground truth is always the full observed record: Weekes & Tomlin (2014a) score 10-year predictions against 10-year observations; Basse et al. (2021) score 90-day-trained corrections against the full measured year; Jonietz Alvarez et al. (2024) score 24-month-based extrapolations against the 24 measured months. Held-out folds are the tool for *short-record* regimes (sub-year training, seasonal-bias risk); once training spans a complete, high-availability year, the seasonal term in Basse's bias equation collapses and evaluation against the whole observed series is the accepted convention. What no within-record scheme can remove is inter-annual variability: 4–10% in wind speed and 6–30% in energy year-to-year (Basse et al. 2021 and refs. therein); the measurement year alone shifts MCP predictions by 1–14% (mean 6.5%) across 12 sites (WES 11:661, 2026); and year-to-year persistence makes even multi-year records overconfident if treated as independent (Bodini et al. 2016 — who also find P50 error stops improving beyond ~4–5 years of record).
- **Gap impact is smaller than the gates imply.** Gottschall & Dörenkämper (2021, WES 6:505, doi:10.5194/wes-6-505-2021): a 30-day gap ≈ 0.01 m/s error in the long-term mean. Jonietz Alvarez et al. (2024): 180-day gaps → < 0.04 m/s mean deviation, < 0.075 m/s p95, so "gap filling does not have the potential to significantly reduce the uncertainty" — 80–90% availability demands look conservative for long-term statistics. Amarzaya (2025, IJRED, doi:10.61435/ijred.2025.61557) similarly reports usable AEP down to ~50% random data recovery (±3.1% best-case relative error) on one Jeju Island dataset.
- **Campaign-length effect:** uncertainty falls monotonically with training length — 1 month already beats ERA5-alone, 12 months is best (Sheridan et al. 2025, 66 US sites); 2–6-year campaigns reduce WRA uncertainty further (Miguel, Fadigas & Sauer 2019, Energies 12(19):3606, doi:10.3390/en12193606, Brazil); rolling one-month start-date sampling is used to quantify uncertainty vs campaign length (Abascal Mendez et al. 2026, Eng. Proc. 11(2):23, https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5134/11/2/23); Amarzaya & Ko 2025 (J. Mech. Sci. Technol., doi:10.1007/s12206-025-0708-1) sweep 1–12 cumulative months and 10–100% recovery with four ML models.

## Recommendations for our protocol

Mapping evidence to the charted protocol (ticket 01). "⚑" marks a tension/contradiction with the charted decision.

1. **Monthly contiguous blocks, never row-level resampling — SUPPORTED.** Blocked/contiguous resampling is the standard correction for autocorrelated data (Künsch 1989; Politis & Romano 1994; Racine 2000; Bergmeir & Benítez 2012; Cerqueira et al. 2020), and every wind MCP benchmark uses contiguous windows sized by season (Weekes & Tomlin 2014a; Basse et al. 2021). Month-length exam blocks match the seasonal dependence scale that actually drives MCP error, not the $n^{1/3}$ variance-estimation optimum — that is the right choice for CV exam periods.
2. **Exam = one month per fold; each month once (≤12 folds) when overlap ≥ 12 months — SUPPORTED, with a reporting caveat.** One-fold-per-calendar-month is the balanced-seasonal-sampling analog of Weekes'/Basse's sliding-window replicates (they used 120/122 *overlapping* windows; non-overlapping monthly folds are the conservative independent-sample version). ⚑ Caveat: folds within one year are not independent replicates (Bodini et al. 2016 — year-to-year persistence); report per-fold metrics and their spread, but do not attach naive SEs/p-values across folds.
3. **≥ 5 equal contiguous folds when overlap < 12 months — NO LITERATURE CONTRADICTION; engineering choice.** No cited study fixes a fold-count floor for non-overlapping MCP folds; the field either uses many overlapping windows (Weekes, Basse) or single train/test splits. Five contiguous folds on a sub-year record keeps every fold a full season-fraction and the train complement multi-seasonal. Note Sheridan et al. (2025): with 1–2-month effective training, a poorly-correlated window can make correction *worse* than no correction — sub-year folds will (correctly) expose this.
4. **Sort by WS RMSE, ε-group (ε = 0.05 m/s), bias secondary, power RMSE only with a configured curve, coverage tertiary — SUPPORTED with one nuance.** Value-by-value RMSE plus distribution/energy statistics is exactly the dual evaluation used in the literature (Jonietz Alvarez et al. 2024); bias as secondary is well-founded because mean bias dominates energy error (~6× variance error; Basse et al. 2021) and is method- and season-dependent with either sign (VR vs OLS-residuals, Basse et al. 2021). Power-curve-gated energy metrics match practice (Rogers 2005; Basse 2021; Sheridan 2025), and Basse showed rankings robust to power-curve choice. Nearest quantitative anchor for ε: across 35 sites the *best-vs-worst* MCP regressions differed by ~0.046 m/s in mean wind speed (Angenent 2023) — ε = 0.05 m/s sits right at "differences smaller than method-level noise", so grouping is defensible; on RMSE (a larger-magnitude statistic) 0.05 m/s is a tight-but-safe tie threshold. ⚑ Nuance, not contradiction: mean-based metrics discriminate MCP methods weakly (Weekes & Tomlin 2014a — 4.8% mean error vs 14% power-density error spread across methods); RMSE-on-speed is more discriminative than mean-bias alone, but where a turbine curve exists the power metric should be surfaced in the recommendation artifact, since the biggest documented method separations (plain OLS vs VR/OLS-with-residuals) live in distribution/energy space.
5. **Whole-series WS RMSE when ≥ 1 yr & ≥ 90% availability — SUPPORTED; both thresholds track normative practice, with one flag.** ≥ 12 complete months is the IEC/MEASNET/FGW normative minimum for on-site data (IEC 61400-1:2019 §11.3.4; MEASNET 2016; FGW 2020; survey in Basse et al. 2021), and whole-record evaluation is the benchmarking convention once training covers the full seasonal cycle. ⚑ The ≥ 90% availability gate is stricter than the evidence on gap impact requires: FGW accepts 80%, and recent WES results show gaps ≤ 180 days shift the long-term mean by < 0.04 m/s (Jonietz Alvarez et al. 2024; Gottschall & Dörenkämper 2021). Keeping 90% is bankable-standard-aligned (MEASNET) and conservative — defensible as the default; consider documenting an FGW-style 80% acceptance for the whole-series path rather than silently dropping otherwise-usable records. Also note whole-series evaluation is in-sample (no held-out protection); the literature always has the long record as ground truth — our position is the same (the observed target series *is* the evaluation target for reconstruction), and production fits retrain on full data regardless (ticket 01 §10).
6. **Method set (OLS 1-sector / OLS 16-sector / VR, WD offset/sectorized) — SUPPORTED; do not presume VR wins.** "No dominant MCP method" is one of the most replicated findings in the field (Rogers et al. 2005; Bass et al. 2000 via Basse 2021; Carta et al. 2013; 2018 German round-robin via Basse 2021; Angenent 2023), which is precisely the justification for benchmarking instead of hardcoding. VR and OLS-with-residuals beat plain OLS on distribution/energy metrics (Weekes & Tomlin 2014a; Rogers et al. 2005) but VR can amplify seasonal mean bias against cycle-biased references (Basse et al. 2021) — so the benchmark must include bias as a live criterion (it does, via the secondary sort). Plain-OLS-without-residuals remains an acceptable mean-predictor; if a downstream consumer needs variance fidelity (e.g., power-curve-sensitive energy), that argues for the ε-group tie-break favoring VR/16-sector, not for deleting OLS. ⚑ Our Julia 16-sector fallback rule (fit sector if ≥ 5 points, else global) is far more permissive than the literature precedent (Weekes & Tomlin 2014a: global fallback below 20 entries per 30° bin), and Basse et al. (2021) found sectorwise fits *worse* for short records from per-sector sparsity. The benchmark's 1-sector vs 16-sector comparison per fold is the right guard; consider raising the per-sector floor or down-weighting 16-sector when overlap < 12 months.
7. **Sectorized (16-sector) training for production fits — SUPPORTED for full-year data.** Sector-wise regression is standard industrial practice for year-long LTCs in complex terrain (Basse et al. 2021; López et al. 2008; Carta et al. 2013; 30° bins in Weekes & Tomlin 2014a and Jonietz Alvarez et al. 2024; SpeedSort per-sector fits, King & Hurley 2005). Our 22.5° bins (16 sectors) are finer than the 30° literature default — consistent with rougher-terrain masts but worth keeping the fallback rule honest (see ⚑ in item 6).
8. **Fold fits are benchmark evidence only; production fits retrain on full data — CONSISTENT.** This mirrors the literature's separation (fold/window replicates for error statistics; one final fit for the delivered correction), and matches the fact that window-averaged regression parameters are not a validated estimator of anything (Basse's bias theory is per-fit, not per-average).

### Contradictions & tensions, explicitly

- **No hard contradiction found** with any charted protocol item. Two soft tensions to record:
  - ⚑ **≥ 90% availability gate vs gap-impact evidence** (item 5): recent WES evidence says availability down to ~75–80% barely moves long-term statistics; our gate follows MEASNET (bankability) not the evidence frontier. Conservative, not wrong.
  - ⚑ **16-sector ≥ 5-point fallback vs literature sparsity precedent** (items 6–7): literature uses ≥ 20 points per 30° bin before trusting a sector fit, and reports sectorwise fits degrading on short records. Our threshold is the aggressive end; rely on the benchmark's sectorization contrast to catch it, or revisit the floor.
- Also worth carrying forward as design context (not contradictions): sub-year single-window MCP can be *worse than no correction* when correlation is weak (Sheridan et al. 2025) — our feasibility screen (ticket 01 §3) plus fold-level metrics cover this; and inter-annual variability (≈ 6.5% mean measurement-year effect; WES 11:661 2026) bounds what any within-record benchmark can resolve.
