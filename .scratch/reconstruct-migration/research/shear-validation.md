## 1. Hold-out-layer validation conventions

### 1.1 Standards: vertical cross-prediction is the sanctioned exam

- **MEASNET (2022). "Evaluation of Site-Specific Wind Conditions", v3.1.** https://www.measnet.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Measnet_Evaluation-of-Site-Especific-Wind-Conditions_v3-1.pdf (mirror used: web.archive.org/web/20240916025642/https://www.measnet.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Measnet_Evaluation-of-Site-Especific-Wind-Conditions_v3-1.pdf). Section 8.4 (verbatim): "The model shall be validated against the available measurement data as far as possible for each application case… This can be done by the cross-prediction and comparison of the wind conditions based on different measurement points (incl. **vertical cross-prediction between different measurement heights** as well as horizontal cross-prediction between different masts)." An explicit standards-body endorsement of the leave-one-height-out exam — a middle-layer hold-out to test interpolation is equally sanctioned. Campaign geometry rules: primary measurement ≥ 2/3 of planned hub height; at least one additional anemometer ≥ 20 m height difference for shear; measurement heights within the complete rotor area "will reduce the uncertainty of the vertical extrapolation". §8.6 lists "Vertical extrapolation" as a named uncertainty-budget component — deviations feed uncertainty, not a pass/fail threshold.
- **IEC 61400-12-1:2017 Ed. 2.0** (webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/26603; free preview: cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/17046/768eb857b82a4b8d99aa87ef901d23ea/IEC-61400-12-1-2017.pdf; superseded by Ed. 3, 2022 +COR1:2025). Power-performance scope; prescribes **no numeric error metric** for extrapolated hub-height speed — its posture is instrumentation + uncertainty combination (Annexes D/E). §7.2.6–7.2.8 define REWS/hub-height wind/shear measurement geometry; Figure 3 "Wind shear measurement heights when no wind speed measurements above hub height are available (for wind shear exponent determination only)" — below-target sensors are recognized as fit for exponent determination, with extrapolation uncertainty carried separately. (Body text paywalled; figure titles verified from the standard's own preview TOC.)

### 1.2 Peer-reviewed hold-out-height exam designs

- **Quick, J., Murcia Leon, J.P., Kock, C.W., Servizi, V., Overgaard, N.S., Dimitrov, N., Kelly, M., Réthoré, P.-E., Kim, T. (2025). "Wind speed vertical extrapolation model validation under uncertainty." Renewable Energy 240, 122028.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2024.122028 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/385601055/1-s2.0-S0960148124020962-main.pdf — full text read this session). Østerild tall mast, 2+ years of 10-min data; log-law fit u = a·ln z + b; **leave-one-out over heights**: for each height, data withheld and the validation metric predicted; "the validation model generally is able to accurately interpolate between height measurements" — the LOO error predictor interpolates reliably inside the bracketed span and is conservative outside it. Compares MAE, RMSE, |bias|, KL divergence and a CDF "area" metric against sanity rules (metric must worsen with larger measurement uncertainty, improve with longer records); finds MAE/RMSE do not reliably decrease with sample count and recommends the area metric or P90 of |bias|. Log-law generally beats power law (consistent with Kelly and Bodini & Optis, whom they cite); both degrade with extrapolation distance. Metrics at 10-min granularity, per height.
- **Bodini, N., Optis, M. (2020). "The importance of round-robin validation when assessing machine-learning-based vertical extrapolation of wind speeds." Wind Energy Science 5(2), 489–501.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-5-489-2020. Train at one site, test at another (round-robin across four SGP sites, 20 months of 30-min data, 143 m target): single-site in-sample validation overstates extrapolation skill — apparent MAE improvements over power-law/log-law baselines shrink substantially under round-robin. The leakage mechanism is memorization of autocorrelated atmospheric state — the same mechanism behind our charted ban on row-level resampling (ticket 01 decision 4).
- **Vassallo, D., Krishnamurthy, R., Fernando, H.J.S. (2021). "Utilizing physics-based input features within a machine learning model to predict wind speed forecasting error." Wind Energy Science 6, 295–307.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-6-295-2021 (open copy: d-nb.info/1228539065/34). Folds must be "split **nearly chronologically**" (stratified contiguous 10-fold CV) precisely because randomly shuffled samples leak autocorrelated atmospheric state — direct primary-source support for blocked/monthly folds. Reports RMSE + MAE per timescale (10 min / 1 h / 3 h).
- **Baquero, L., Torío, H., Leask, P. (2022). "Machine Learning Algorithms for Vertical Wind Speed Data Extrapolation: Comparison and Performance Using Mesoscale and Measured Site Data." Energies 15(15), 5518.** https://doi.org/10.3390/en15155518. Industrial-pattern exam on a real mast: 50 m + 75 m as inputs, the 102.2 m level withheld as validation target (R² ≈ 0.97 vs ≈ 0.63 mesoscale-only) — the top-height hold-out in its purest commercial form.
- **Basse, A., Pauscher, L., Callies, D. (2020). "Improving Vertical Wind Speed Extrapolation Using Short-Term LiDAR Measurements." Remote Sensing 12(7), 1091.** https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12071091. Documents that the mast-top shear exponent αL ≠ αH above the mast (Rα typically < 1, so using αL overestimates when extrapolating upward; large gaps like 80→200 m are worst) — quantifies how extrapolation skill degrades with distance above the top sensor.
- **Supporting (first-party tool docs):** Windographer ships the exam as a feature — "test to see how well the extrapolation process, using all but the top measurement height on a tall met tower, predicts the measurements made at the top of the tower" (support.ul-renewables.com/windographer/dlg-revise-vertical-extrapolation-test.htm). IEC 61400-15-2 NP §7.1.9 requires the WRA report to describe "the process by which wind speed is extrapolated (or in some cases interpolated) vertically from sensor height(s) to the turbine hub height" (aresca.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/88_1038e_NP.pdf — snippet-verified, document not fetchable here).

**Conventions synthesis:** (i) withhold the top layer for extrapolation exams / a middle layer for interpolation exams — both are "vertical cross-prediction" under MEASNET §8.4; (ii) report bias and MAE/RMSE in m/s at native 10-min granularity, per height (and per direction sector where sectorized methods compete), with a distributional check (CDF-area or P90|bias|) since Quick et al. show MAE/RMSE alone don't reward longer records; (iii) no standards body sets a numeric acceptance threshold — evidence feeds an uncertainty budget; (iv) folds must be contiguous/chronological (blocked), never row-level shuffles.

## 2. Shear time variability: which aggregation carries signal vs noise

### 2.1 Diurnal and seasonal structure of α is strong, systematic signal

- **Yang, X., Jiang, X., Liang, S., Qin, Y., Ye, F., Ye, B., Xu, J., He, X., Wu, J., Dong, T., Cai, X., Xu, R., Zeng, Z. (2024). "Spatiotemporal variation of power law exponent on the use of wind energy." Applied Energy 356, 122441.** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306261923018056 (DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2023.122441). Large-sample study of the power-law exponent α across time and space; documents that α is systematically smaller during daytime (convective, well-mixed) and larger at night (stable stratification), with clear seasonal and regional structure. The diurnal pattern is a repeatable, predictable signal, not noise.
- **Yan, B.W., Li, Q.S., Chan, P.W., He, Y.C., Shu, Z.R. (2022). "Characterising wind shear exponents in the offshore area using Lidar measurements." Applied Ocean Research 123, 103293.** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0141118722002279. Offshore lidar study: α decreases during daytime when surface heating drives unstable conditions and increases at night; seasonal variations of α are significant; the default 1/7 power law is often misleading because α depends on stability, height range, and season.
- **Barthelmie, R.J., Pryor, S.C. (2020). "Power and Wind Shear Implications of Large Wind Turbine Scenarios in the US Central Plains." Energies 13(16), 4269.** https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/13/16/4269. WRF-based climatology: shear exponent shows a marked diurnal cycle at all locations (lowest during day), and mean α varies with hub height and rotor size scenario — the "right" α depends on the height range it is fitted over.
- **Crippa, P., Alifa, M., Bolster, D., Genton, M.G., Castruccio, S. (2021). "A temporal model for vertical extrapolation of wind speed and wind energy assessment." Applied Energy 301, 117378.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2021.117378 (author PDF: marcgenton.github.io/2021.CABGC.APEN.pdf). Models α as an explicit function of hour of day (harmonic temporal model); the time-varying α model reduces wind-speed RMSE by 23–33% domain-wide relative to constant-shear extrapolation. Direct evidence that sub-daily (hourly-scale) aggregation of shear carries real predictive signal.
- **Zhou, L., Esau, I. (2026). "Determining the ideal length of wind speed series for wind speed distribution and resource assessment." Wind Energy Science 11, 217–240.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-11-217-2026. Basic wind statistics (mean, std, Weibull parameters) stabilize with roughly one month of hourly data sampled across years; higher moments need years; structure-preserving (continuous) sampling behaves differently from random sampling. Implication: monthly-scale fits already capture most of the stable climatology, and whole-series refits beyond ~1 year add little precision to mean-level parameters while retaining zero time structure.

### 2.2 Per-timestamp α is noisy; conditioning — not finer resolution — is what improves skill

- **Optis, M., Bodini, N., Debnath, M., Doubrawa, P. (2021). "New methods to improve the vertical extrapolation of near-surface offshore wind speeds." Wind Energy Science 6, 935–948.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-6-935-2021. Evaluates four families of extrapolation methods (power law and log law included, each also in internally time-averaged variants) against buoy and lidar observations; the power law "requires measurements at two heights to calculate the shear coefficient", and simply carrying a per-timestamp α adds noise — averaging/conditioning is embedded in the better-performing variants. Their benchmark holds out validation heights and reports binned metrics — the same leave-one-height-out shape as our exam.
- **Machidon, D. et al. (2024). "Wind Shear Coefficient Estimation Based on LIDAR Measurements to Improve Power Law Extrapolation Performance." Remote Sensing 17(1), 23.** https://doi.org/10.3390/rs17010023. 10-minute WSCs from five lidar heights are computed and then averaged (fixed-reference vs successive-reference definitions); averaged WSC conditioned on height pairing beats naive use, and time-varying/stability-aware WSC beats a constant α. Confirms 10-min point estimates are usable but aggregation/conditioning is where accuracy comes from.
- **Kelly, M. (2016). "Uncertainty in vertical extrapolation of wind statistics: shear-exponent and WAsP/EWA methods." DTU Wind Energy report E-0121.** https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/126254660/VertExtrapUncert_Kelly2016_DTU_en_.pdf. Derives the uncertainty of a mean shear exponent and its propagation to extrapolated mean wind speed; explicitly specifies the mean α **per directional sector** so that extrapolation is probability-weighted by sector — the industry-uncertainty framework conditions α on direction and uses long-term-mean α, not instantaneous α. For small height ratios, shear-exponent and WAsP/EWA extrapolation have similar uncertainty; for larger ratios WAsP/EWA (stability-aware log law) tends to have smaller uncertainty.
- **Kelly, M., Troen, I., Jørgensen, H.E. (2014). "Weibull-k Revisited: 'Tall' Profiles and Height Variation of Wind Statistics." Boundary-Layer Meteorology 152, 461–472.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10546-014-9915-5. Height variation of wind statistics over tall profiles deviates from surface-layer similarity above the surface layer — supporting sector/height-conditional treatment and caution with a single global α.
- **Kelly, M., Jørgensen, H.E. (2017). "Statistical characterization of roughness uncertainty and impact on wind resource estimation." Wind Energy Science 2, 189–197.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-2-189-2017. Companion uncertainty analysis: log-law roughness parameter uncertainty propagates into hub-height speed and AEP — log-law-family fits carry parameter uncertainty comparable to α-based ones.

### 2.3 Stability conditioning explains much of the variability

- **Optis, M., Monahan, A., Bosveld, F.C. (2016). "Limitations and breakdown of Monin–Obukhov similarity theory for wind profile extrapolation under stable stratification." Wind Energy 19, 1053–1072.** https://doi.org/10.1002/we.1883. MOST-based extrapolation degrades/breaks down under stable stratification — exactly the night-time high-α regimes; a single unconditional profile law cannot cover the stability range.
- **Xu, C., Hao, C., Li, L., Han, X., Xue, F., et al. (2018). "Evaluation of the Power-Law Wind-Speed Extrapolation Method with Atmospheric Stability Classification Methods for Flows over Different Terrain Types." Applied Sciences 8(9), 1429.** https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/8/9/1429 (PDF: res.mdpi.com/d_attachment/applsci/applsci-08-01429/article_deploy/applsci-08-01429-v2.pdf). Power-law extrapolation to hub height evaluated with stability classification across terrain types; accuracy improves when α is conditioned on stability class; unclassified α produces the largest errors.
- **Liu, H. et al. (2024). "Wind Shear Model Considering Atmospheric Stability to Improve Accuracy of Wind Resource Assessment." Processes 12(5), 954.** https://doi.org/10.3390/pr12050954. Extrapolation from 50–150 m to hub heights with stability-aware WSC improves accuracy over unconditional WSC.
- **Hsu, S.A., Meindl, E.A., Gilhousen, D.B. (1994). "Determining the Power-Law Wind-Profile Exponent under Near-Neutral Stability Conditions at Sea." Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology 33(6), 757–765.** https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1994)033<0757:DTPLWP>2.0.CO;2. Origin of the near-neutral α ≈ 1/7 default; valid only for near-neutral marine conditions — the reason a fixed default underperforms on land.

### 2.4 Aggregation scale: signal-vs-noise synthesis

- **Yang, X., et al. (2024). "Time resolution of wind speed data introduces errors in wind power density assessment." Energy Conversion and Management: X 24, 100753.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecmx.2024.100753. The temporal resolution of wind-speed data itself changes derived statistics: aggregating data changes the shear statistics you infer — fit timescale and evaluation timescale interact and must be reported.
- **Li, J., et al. (2018). "Use of spatio-temporal calibrated wind shear model to improve accuracy of wind resource assessment." Applied Energy.** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0306261918300606. Two-year lidar calibration of power-law/log-law shear parameters at 10-minute resolution; site-specific spatio-temporal calibration beats defaults; at their site the power law extrapolated more accurately than the log law.

**Synthesis (which aggregation carries signal vs noise):**
- *10-min / per-timestamp α*: highest variance, autocorrelated, noisy point estimates — but unbiased representation of the diurnal/stability signal if a conditioned model consumes it. As a *direct* predictor of the held-out layer it inherits measurement noise twice (fit + reference).
- *1h*: near-identical signal content to 10-min (turbulent noise averages out; diurnal cycle intact) at a fraction of the autocorrelation burden — the de-facto standard resolution in the literature above.
- *day*: weakest justification — destroys the diurnal cycle (documented as the dominant α signal) while retaining synoptic noise; no cited practice fits α at daily aggregation for prediction.
- *month*: captures seasonality; mean/std-level statistics already stabilize around this scale (Zhou & Esau 2026); sector-conditional monthly α is the closest analogue to the Kelly/DTU uncertainty framework's recommended conditioning.
- *whole-series*: most stable (lowest estimation noise) but no time structure at all; constant-α extrapolation is the baseline every temporal-model paper beats by 23–33% RMSE (Crippa et al. 2021).

## 3. Profile fitting: layer selection and minimum heights

### 3.1 All heights vs heights nearest the extrapolation target

- **Kelly, M., Kersting, G., Mazoyer, P., Yang, C., Hernández Fillols, F., Clark, S., Matos, J.C. (2019). "Uncertainty in vertical extrapolation of measured wind speed via shear." DTU Wind Energy E-0195.** https://doi.org/10.11581/dtu.00000261 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/215202442/ShearExtrapolUnc_DTUe0195_Jun2020.pdf — read directly). For ≥3 heights, α by least-squares in {ln z, ln V}; but verbatim warning: "use of more than 2 heights can either decrease or increase the uncertainty, particularly because **lower measurement heights tend to contribute less representative information with regard to hub-height prediction**". Defines the *effective observation height* z_obs = exp[Σ ln z_i / n] (weighted geometric mean) on which representativeness uncertainty σ_rep ∝ α·ln(z_pred/z_obs) depends — pulling the fit down with low sensors inflates extrapolation error upward. Crude multi-height variance adjustment ×√(2/n); fit uncertainty σ_fit by Monte Carlo per JCGM 101; systematic bias propagates as a separate √2·b·ln(z2/z₁)-type term. Per-height weights w_i explicitly supported ("replace 1/n with the individual weights w_i") — the only standards-adjacent endorsement of height weighting found.
- **Wagner, R., Courtney, M., Gottschall, J., Lindelöw-Marsden, P. (2011). "Accounting for the speed shear in wind turbine power performance measurement." Wind Energy 14(8), 993–1004.** https://doi.org/10.1002/we.509 (author-hosted copy: breeze.colorado.edu/ATOC6020_2012Spring/Welcome_files/Wagner_etal2011WE_powercurve.pdf). Deriving α from hub height and below and assuming continuity above is "not always wise since profiles with a local extremum or high shear above hub height cannot be adequately represented by a power law"; a nine-height α_fit is "more representative … than a shear exponent retrieved from only two measurement points". Their per-timestamp fit is anchored at the hub-height point (u_fit(z) = u_hub·(z/z_hub)^α) — the anchor-at-target variant.
- **MEASNET (2016). "Evaluation of Site-Specific Wind Conditions" v2.0.** https://web.archive.org/web/20170713034221/http://www.measnet.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Measnet_SiteAssessment_V2.0.pdf (read directly). §9.6: allows "a fit through all the measurement heights" when >2 heights exist, but "for the most accurate determination of α, measurement heights should be close to upper blade tip or hub height and to the lower blade tip"; §7.2: primary level ≥ 2/3 hub height because heights closer to hub "reduce the uncertainty of the vertical extrapolation".
- **IEC 61400-15-1:2025 "Site suitability input conditions for wind power plants", Ed. 1.0, clause 5.5.** https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/29169 (FDIS text read: aresca.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/88_1041e_FDIS.pdf). "The measurement heights used for calculation shall be chosen to best represent the wind shear exponent from bottom tip to tip height. If the measurement heights are not representative for the shear across the rotor area, consider using a power law wind profile or other applicable wind profiles that describe the rotor swept area." Also defines two normative averaging paths: per-10-min α grouped by direction sector with arithmetic (frequency-weighted) mean — noted as approximation to the energy-weighted average required by IEC 61400-1:2019 — or temporally averaged concurrent speeds fitted with the profile; plus displacement-height (z−d) correction over forestry/obstacles.
- **Gryning, S.-E., Batchvarova, E., Brümmer, B., Jørgensen, H., Larsen, S. (2007). "On the extension of the wind profile over homogeneous terrain beyond the surface boundary layer." Boundary-Layer Meteorology 124(2), 251–268.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10546-007-9166-9 (record: orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/on-the-extension-of-the-wind-profile-over-homogeneous-terrain-bey/). 160 m (Høvsøre) and 250 m (Hamburg) tower data: above ~50–80 m the real wind becomes systematically *larger* than the surface-layer log/MO profile predicts — a fit dominated by surface-layer heights is structurally biased for extrapolation above the mast top.
- **Kelly, M., van der Laan, M.P. (2023).** (section 4 below): the power law is only "assumed to be valid over some extent around height z_ref" — local validity, not mast-wide.

### 3.2 Minimum-height thresholds and surface-layer distortion

- **Gryning et al. (2007)** (above) + **Gryning, S.-E., Batchvarova, E., Brümmer, B., Emeis, S., Gulstad, L., Lee, N. (2010). "The Tall Wind project — wind profile and boundary-layer height." ISARS 2010.** https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/5117521/Gryning_the%20tall%20wind%20project.pdf (read directly). The M–O surface-layer profile holds only to ~50–80 m over land; above it the profile depends on boundary-layer depth h; "the wind speed becomes larger as compared to the traditionally used logarithmic wind profile".
- **Kelly, M.C., Troen, I. (2016). "Probabilistic stability and 'tall' wind profiles: theory and method for use in wind resource assessment." Wind Energy 19(2), 227–241.** https://doi.org/10.1002/we.1829 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/123724712/Probabilistic_stability.pdf). Surface layer = "below 1/10th of the atmospheric boundary layer depth, which can be shallower than 200 m at night" — i.e. ~20 m on stable nights — motivating a model for heights "above the atmospheric surface layer (z ≳ 50 m)".
- **Optis, M., Monahan, A., Bosveld, F.C. (2016). "Limitations and breakdown of Monin–Obukhov similarity theory for wind profile extrapolation under stable stratification." Wind Energy 19, 1053–1072.** https://doi.org/10.1002/we.1883 (companion: Optis, Monahan, Bosveld 2014, Boundary-Layer Meteorology, doi:10.1007/s10546-014-9953-z). Ten years of Cabauw 213 m tower data: MOST extrapolation breaks down above the surface layer under stable stratification (diverging stability functions, Coriolis/decoupling effects).
- **Kelly, M.C. (2016). DTU Wind Energy E-0121.** (section 2 above; read directly). Measurement heights should be "located within the same atmospheric regime (e.g. above the surface layer at night/in shallow ABLs …)"; "for subdivision of data into winter/night-time, the heights should all be above ~30 m"; heights must not be "smaller than the local scale of terrain elevation changes"; log-law/WAsP extrapolation capped at z_pred/z_obs ≲ 2.

**Threshold synthesis:** no single universal cutoff, but three converging rules: (1) physical ceiling of the surface layer ≈ max(0.1·h_ABL, ~50–80 m at flat-land sites per Høvsøre/Hamburg/Cabauw evidence); (2) Kelly's operational floor of ~30 m for stable/nighttime subsets, higher in complex terrain (above local terrain-elevation scale, plus displacement height over forest); (3) IEC-world practice: ≥40 m minimum masts, ≥2/3-hub primary sensors, and RSD height selection by heights "closest to the referring mast measurement height and the target height" (MEASNET v2 Annex C.10 — proximity weighting in practice). For a 10–160 m mast extrapolating upward, excluding 10–40 m sensors from the extrapolation fit is defensible from every source; a 40–60 m inclusion threshold (dynamically raised under stable conditions) is the literature-consistent default. The `>50m` layer scheme in our matrix sits squarely inside this range.

## 4. Wind-direction veer modeling and circular metrics

### 4.1 Veer physics and magnitudes

- **Kelly, M., van der Laan, M.P. (2023). "From shear to veer: theory, statistics, and practical application." Wind Energy Science 8, 975–993.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-8-975-2023. The theory+statistics reference for veer in wind energy: veer ∂φ/∂z depends mainly on height and ABL depth (not roughness) within typical wind-energy regimes; shear and veer are jointly related to stability across sites (Høvsøre, Østerild, Cabauw, ridge-top) with quasi-universal empirical relations — a fitted shear model already encodes much of the veer signal, but the mapping is stability-dependent, not constant.
- **Abkar, M., Porté-Agel, F. (2014). "The turning of the wind in the atmospheric boundary layer." J. Phys.: Conf. Ser. 524, 012118.** https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-6596/524/1/012118 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/93522281/The_turning_of_the_wind.pdf). Høvsøre tall-mast + lidar case studies: ~43° of veer within the first 100 m under very stable nocturnal conditions; ~25° (neutral) to ~45° (stable) across the whole ABL under barotropic conditions. Magnitudes are strongly regime-dependent — a single constant veer offset is a crude average over a wide distribution.
- **Sanchez Gomez, M., Lundquist, J.K. (2020). "The effect of wind direction shear on turbine performance in a wind farm in central Iowa." Wind Energy Science 5, 125–140.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-5-125-2020. Direction shear (veer) and speed shear co-vary through a marked diurnal cycle; clockwise veering predominates and is the more damaging regime — veer is systematically conditioned on time of day/stability and direction, not uniform.
- **Murphy, P., Lundquist, J.K., Fleming, P. (2020). "How wind speed shear and directional veer affect the power production of a megawatt-scale operational wind turbine." Wind Energy Science 5, 1169–1190.** https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-5-1169-2020. Quantifies rotor-disk veer metrics (βbulk, βtotal) and their power impact (~5% of rated power at their High Plains site) — establishes that veer magnitude matters energetically and defines the standard bulk-veer measurement convention (direction difference across a height span).

### 4.2 Modeling practice: constant offset vs conditioned; circular metrics

- **Kelly, M., et al. (2023). "Using Observed and Modelled Heat Fluxes for Improved Extrapolation of Wind Distributions." Boundary-Layer Meteorology.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10546-023-00803-3. WAsP's height-transfer machinery is explicitly **sector-wise**: distributions are transferred per wind-direction sector with stability parameters, and direction-dependent corrections are part of the transfer — the reference wind-resource stack conditions direction handling on sector, not on a single global offset.
- **Bao, L., Gneiting, T., Grimit, E.P., Guttorp, P., Raftery, A.E. (2010). "Bias correction and Bayesian model averaging for ensemble forecasts of surface wind direction." Monthly Weather Review 138(5), 1811–1821.** https://doi.org/10.1175/2009MWR3138.1. The canonical treatment of wind direction as a circular variable in statistical post-processing: bias correction via circular–circular regression (location-specific), von Mises components for BMA; simple scalar offsets underperform once training data grows. Directly analogous to WD-extrapolate: direction bias is location/regime-conditioned and must be computed with circular statistics.
- **Jammalamadaka, S.R., SenGupta, A. (2001). "Topics in Circular Statistics." World Scientific.** https://www.worldscientific.com/worldscibooks/10.1142/4031. Defines the standard circular distance d(α,β) = 1 − cos(α−β) ∈ [0,2], the basis of the "accuracy in degrees" metric; the R `circular` package (cran.r-project.org/web/packages/circular/) implements the canonical estimators (circular mean via atan2 of mean sin/cos, APE-style errors).

**Metrics practice synthesis:** the forecast-verification literature evaluates direction predictions on **wrapped angular differences**: MAE_θ = mean |wrap(θ−θ̂)| with wrap to (−180°, 180°]; RMSE_θ analogously; or the circular form d̄ = 1 − mean cos(θ−θ̂) converted to degrees via arccos(1 − d̄) — all reduce to "compute error on the shortest arc". Circularity of the *fit* side matters too: a sector-mean veer offset must be computed as a circular mean of direction differences, never an arithmetic mean of raw angles.

## 5. Interpolation between bracketing heights

- **MEASNET (2022), §9.4 "Determination of the Vertical Wind Shear".** https://www.measnet.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Measnet_Evaluation-of-Site-Especific-Wind-Conditions_v3-1.pdf. Normative kernel: shear between two heights uses the power law, α = ln(V1/V2)/ln(z1/z2), computed **per 10-minute step**, binned by 1 m/s and ≤30° direction sectors, >3 m/s, energy/frequency-weighted; with >2 heights, α from "a fit through all the measurement heights, respectively the wind shear exponents for different height intervals" — **per-interval shear is explicitly anticipated by the standard** (ticket 08's per-height-interval parameters have a normative antecedent). Also: "for the most accurate determination of α, measurement heights should be close to upper blade tip or hub height and to the lower blade tip"; "only the measurement heights which are most representative for the rotor swept area shall be used".
- **Wagner, R., Cañadillas, B., Clifton, A., Feeney, S., Nygaard, N., Poodt, M., St. Martin, C., Tüxen, E., Wagenaar, J.W. (2014). "Rotor equivalent wind speed for power curve measurement — comparative exercise for IEA Wind Annex 32." J. Phys.: Conf. Ser. 524, 012108.** https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-6596/524/1/012108 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/93592493/Rotor_equivalent_wind_speed.pdf). Eight organizations, Høvsøre 40–116 m profile with strong shear (6.05→11.46 m/s): the full spread of profile treatments — measured values with midpoint segments (9.38 m/s), linear interpolation to a uniform grid (9.40 m/s), equal-height segments (9.35 m/s) — moved the rotor-averaged speed by ≲0.5%. Consensus verbatim: use the measured profile and "avoid interpolation or extrapolation because they increase the uncertainty" (midpoint-segment rule carried into IEC 61400-12-1:2017 §7.2.6).
- **Kelly, M., Kersting, G., Mazoyer, P., Yang, C., Hernández Fillols, F., Clark, S., Matos, J.C. (2019). "Uncertainty in vertical extrapolation of measured wind speed via shear." DTU Wind Energy Report E-0195.** https://doi.org/10.11581/dtu.00000261 (open PDF: backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/215202442/ShearExtrapolUnc_DTUe0195_Jun2020.pdf). σ_α² = σ²_repr + σ²_prop + σ²_fit; the dominant representativity term grows like α·ln(z_pred/z_obs) **outside** the measured span — inside the span (interpolation) it is intrinsically small. The propagated-measurement term carries a 1/ln(z2/z1) factor, so wider bracketing heights reduce fit uncertainty; multi-height fits reduce it by ~√(2/n). Cleanest primary-source basis for "interpolation error ≪ extrapolation error". Industrial practice per the report: α computed per 10-minute interval from the bracketing pair; 10 min is the standard averaging window (>1 h violates stationarity).
- **windpowerlib documentation — `tools.logarithmic_interpolation_extrapolation()`.** https://windpowerlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/temp/windpowerlib.tools.logarithmic_interpolation_extrapolation.html (method from Knorr, K. (2016), Dissertation Univ. Kassel, p. 83). Reference open-source implementation: log-height interpolation between the two closest heights, f(x) = [ln x·(f(x2)−f(x1)) − f(x2)·ln x1 + f(x1)·ln x2]/(ln x2 − ln x1); plain linear available but non-default. DTU windkit agrees: `windkit.vinterp_wind_speed(..., log_height=True)` — "interpolate in log-height space. Defaults to True" (docs.wasp.dk/windkit/v1.0.2/api_reference/wind_funcs_autogen/windkit.vinterp_wind_speed.html). Both reference Python ecosystems default to log-height interpolation between bracketing heights.
- **Van Sark, W.G.J.H.M., Van der Velde, H.C., Coelingh, J.P., Bierbooms, W.A.A.M. (2019). "Do we really need rotor equivalent wind speed?" Wind Energy 22(5), 745–763.** https://doi.org/10.1002/we.2319. For D/H < 1.8 and shear coefficients −0.05…0.4, hub-height speed suffices — intra-span profile treatment is second-order. Corroborates that interpolation error over typical mast spans is small relative to above-top extrapolation error.
- **Quick et al. (2025)** (section 1): the LOO validation-metric curve "interpolates between height measurements" reliably and is conservative outside them — empirical confirmation of the same asymmetry on a real mast.

**Honesty note:** no verified peer-reviewed study directly tabulates log-linear vs two-point power-law vs linear-in-z interpolation errors across a 40–160 m span; the magnitude evidence is assembled from the Annex 32 exercise (≲0.5% spread across profile treatments), Kelly et al. (2019) uncertainty scalings, and the windpowerlib/windkit defaults. The two shear-law forms are near-equivalent over typical spans in moderate shear (both are 2-parameter curves through the same two points; they differ only in curvature between them, which shrinks with the span).

## Recommendations for our protocol

Item-by-item mapping of evidence to the charted protocol (tickets 01/07/08). Contradictions flagged inline with **[CONTRADICTION]**; gaps with **[GAP]**.

### R1. WS-extrapolate candidate matrix (5 time scales × 4 layer schemes = 20) — trim to 16

**Time scales — trim "day":**

- *10-min and 1h*: keep both. The diurnal cycle is the dominant, predictable α signal (Yang 2024; Yan 2022; Barthelmie & Pryor 2020), and an hour-of-day-conditioned α beats constant α by 23–33% RMSE (Crippa et al. 2021). 1h carries near-identical signal to 10-min with less noise/autocorrelation burden; the resolution interaction itself is documented (Yang 2024, ECM:X). Keep both as distinct candidates only because fits are cheap; expect them to near-tie inside the ε-group.
- *day*: **drop**. It destroys the diurnal cycle (the documented dominant signal) while retaining synoptic-scale noise, and no cited primary source fits α at daily aggregation for prediction. The only scale of the five with no literature anchor.
- *month*: keep — captures seasonality; mean-level statistics stabilize around monthly scales (Zhou & Esau 2026); closest analogue to the sector-wise long-term-mean α of the DTU/IEC uncertainty framework (Kelly 2016).
- *whole-series*: keep — the stable, zero-time-structure baseline; the constant-α control every temporal model beats; the benchmark needs it to quantify what time structure buys per mast.

**Layer schemes — keep all four:**

- *topmost nearest 3*: anchored by MEASNET ("only the measurement heights which are most representative for the rotor swept area shall be used"; heights "close to … hub height and … lower blade tip") and by Kelly et al. (2019)'s warning that lower heights contribute less representative information for upper prediction.
- *distance-weighted shear profile fitting*: the only scheme with an explicit normative endorsement of per-height weights (Kelly et al. 2019: "replace 1/n with the individual weights w_i"); weighting directly manipulates the effective observation height z_obs that controls σ_rep ∝ α·ln(z_pred/z_obs).
- *all >50m*: squarely inside the literature's surface-layer exclusion band (Gryning 2007: log/MO profile holds only to ~50–80 m; Kelly & Troen 2016: z ≳ 50 m above ASL; Kelly E-0121: ≥30 m floor for stable/night subsets; IEC-world ≥40 m practice).
- *all heights*: the control that quantifies what surface-layer exclusion buys; also the variant Wagner et al. (2011) found "more representative" when the whole span sits within the regime of interest.

→ **4 scales × 4 schemes = 16 candidates.** The benchmark, not this survey, picks winners per mast; the trim only removes the scale with no evidentiary support.

**[CONTRADICTION] power law vs log law:** peer-reviewed comparisons repeatedly find the log-law/stability-aware family at least as accurate as — and generally better than — the plain power law (Quick et al. 2025 at Østerild; Bodini & Optis 2020; Optis et al. 2021 offshore), while MEASNET §9.4 mandates the power-law kernel and IEC 61400-15-1:2025 permits "other applicable wind profiles". The charted matrix varies scale × layers but fixes the law family. Recommendation: register the law family as a method dimension (power-law α and log-linear u = a·ln z + b as sibling methods per scale × scheme where ≥3 heights are fitted), or at minimum accept log-linear for interpolation (R4). Otherwise the benchmark cannot detect the documented log-law advantage.

**[GAP] direction-sector conditioning:** MEASNET/IEC practice conditions α on ≤30° direction sectors (frequency/energy-weighted), and Kelly's uncertainty framework specifies mean α per sector; our matrix varies time scale × layers but not sector. Do not explode the matrix now — but plan a follow-up candidate "winner + per-sector refit" so the benchmark can expose whether sector conditioning matters at our masts.

### R2. Exam design (ticket 07)

- **Hold out the top WS/WD layer** for extrapolation exams, **a middle layer** for interpolation exams — both are sanctioned as "vertical cross-prediction between different measurement heights" (MEASNET §8.4) and match Quick et al. (2025)'s LOO-over-heights and Windographer's shipped test. Predict at native 10-min resolution.
- **Monthly blocked bootstrap** matches the leakage evidence: contiguous/chronological folds because shuffled rows leak autocorrelated atmospheric state (Vassallo et al. 2021; Bodini & Optis 2020). Round-robin across masts is the stronger variant when multiple masts share overlap — optional future evidence, not required.
- **Metrics**: keep the charted sort (WS RMSE ascending, ε-grouping, bias secondary) — it matches common practice (RMSE/MAE/bias in m/s at 10-min, per height). **[CONTRADICTION-lite]** Quick et al. (2025) show MAE/RMSE do not monotonically reward longer records and recommend a distributional metric (CDF-area or P90|bias|) — do not displace the decided sort, but record P90|bias| per candidate in the benchmark evidence as a diagnostic the ε-group tie-break can consult.
- **Winner granularity (ticket 07 Q1)**: evidence favors per (mast × target height): α depends on the height range fitted (Barthelmie & Pryor 2020), the effective observation height z_obs is target-relative (Kelly et al. 2019), and MEASNET anticipates per-interval exponents. A per-mast winner should at minimum be validated per target height before reuse.
- **Feasibility screen input (ticket 09)**: extrapolation reliability degrades with ln(height ratio); log-law/WAsP practice caps z_pred/z_obs ≲ 2 (Kelly E-0121) and Basse et al. (2020) show mast-top α ≠ above-mast α with error growing with the gap. A height-ratio guard belongs in the screen.

### R3. WD-extrapolate (ticket 07)

- **Candidate set**: {constant circular offset} × {global, per-sector-of-reference-direction} × {1h, month, whole-series} — 6 candidates; no 10-min variant (veer point estimates inherit direction-sensor noise twice, and the veer signal is diurnal/stability-driven — the offset scale captures it without per-timestamp fitting). Veer magnitudes are large and regime-dependent (~25–45° across the ABL, up to ~43° in the first 100 m in very stable nights — Abkar & Porté-Agel 2014; diurnal co-variation with shear — Sanchez Gomez & Lundquist 2020), so per-sector conditioning is expected to win at most sites; the WAsP stack and circular-regression practice are both sector/regime-conditioned (Kelly et al. 2023 BLM; Bao et al. 2010).
- **Metric**: circular MAE on wrapped differences — MAE_θ = mean |wrap(θ−θ̂)|, wrap to (−180°, 180°] — as primary (robust, interpretable degrees); circular RMSE_θ as tie-break; offsets always fitted as circular means of direction differences (Jammalamadaka & SenGupta 2001; never arithmetic means of raw angles).
- **Selection independence**: select WD independently of WS. Veer affects power (~5%-of-rated at rotor scale, Murphy et al. 2020) but WS dominates reconstruction error; no literature supports a scalar joint WS+WD objective — don't invent one.

### R4. Interpolation method set (ticket 08)

- **WS-interpolate**: accept **log-linear in ln(z) between bracketing heights per timestamp as the default without a full benchmark**, with the **two-point power law as the single benchmarked competitor** (middle-layer hold-out, same blocked folds; expected to near-tie). Justification: both reference open-source stacks default to log-height interpolation (windpowerlib per Knorr 2016; DTU windkit `log_height=True`); MEASNET §9.4's normative kernel is the two-point power law; the two forms are near-equivalent 2-point curves; the full spread of profile treatments over a strong-shear 40–116 m span moved rotor-averaged speed ≲0.5% (Wagner et al. 2014). **Drop plain linear-in-z** — non-default in both reference tools and the discouraged minority option in Annex 32.
- **Per-interval parameters**: yes — MEASNET §9.4 explicitly anticipates "the wind shear exponents for different height intervals"; carry per-interval parameters in one fit artifact (one fit covering all intervals, each interval with its own α or a,b fitted from its bracketing pair), not one artifact per interval.
- **WD-interpolate**: circular-linear interpolation between bracketing heights weighted by ln(z), explicit shortest-arc wrap handling; consistent with R3's circular-metric practice.
- **Exam**: interpolation candidates get the middle-layer hold-out spot-check (sanctioned by MEASNET vertical cross-prediction), not the full bootstrap ceremony — interpolation error is dominated by extrapolation error (σ_rep grows with ln(z_pred/z_obs) only outside the measured span — Kelly et al. 2019; Quick et al. 2025's metric interpolates reliably inside spans; van Sark et al. 2019 shows intra-span treatment is second-order at moderate shear). This matches the user steer "interpolation is normally not complex".
- **Boundary composition (ticket 07 Q3 / 08 Q5)**: no literature prescribes the compose rule; the evidence-consistent choice is per-target-height routing — interpolation fits for gaps between measured layers, the extrapolation fit above the top layer — with the extrapolation reference height at the top sensor (matching Windographer/Wagner anchor practice and Kelly's z_obs convention).

### R5. What the literature does NOT support (avoid)

- No numeric acceptance threshold for extrapolated hub-height speed exists in IEC/MEASNET — the benchmark recommends; it does not certify.
- No support for row-level (random) resampling of autocorrelated wind data — already banned by ticket 01 decision 4; corroborated by Bodini & Optis 2020 and Vassallo et al. 2021.
- No support for daily-aggregated α fitting (dropped in R1).
- No verified direct comparison table of interpolation-law errors over 40–160 m spans (section 5 honesty note) — hence "default + one competitor" rather than a 3-way race.
